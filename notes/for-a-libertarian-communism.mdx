#### The French Revolution De-Jacobinized

- [sans-culottes ('without breeches')](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sans-culottes): the "common people of the lower classes in 18-century France" - the radical folks!

> The great lesson of 1793 is not just that direct democracy is viable, it is also that the vanguard of a society, when it is still a minority in relation to the mass of the country that it carries along with it, cannot avoid, in that life-or-death battle that is a revolution, imposing its will on the majority, first and preferentially through persuasion, and, if persuasion fails, by force. 

- Interesting bit about the "dictatorship of the proletariat" phrase used by Marx and Engels: "it was never truly elaborated by its authors", and apparently [Kautsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Kautsky) claimed that in their work it was just a _Wörtchen_, "a little word occasionally used but of no importance."

> In fact, for the men of the eighteenth century,... dictatorship had a precise and formidable meaning. They recalled ... that the Romans, “having driven out their kings saw themselves obliged, in difficult times, to temporarily create a dictator enjoying greater power than any enjoyed by the ancient kings.” They recalled that later, the institution having degenerated, Sulla and Caesar proclaimed themselves perpetual dictators and exercised absolute sovereignty... They did not want either a new monarch or a new Caesar. 

> Finally, the rank-and-file sans-culottes, the men and women of the popular societies, instinctively distrusted the word dictatorship, for it would have represented only a portion of revolutionary reality: they first of all wanted to convince, to open to everyone the doors to the nascent democracy, and they only resorted to force when those they wanted to convince and admit into democracy answered them with lead. 

- The Babouvists (followers of [Babeuf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois-No%C3%ABl_Babeuf)) were the first to speak of revolutionary dictatorship. "A minuscule and isolated minority, they doubted the people’s capacity to lead themselves, at least in the near term, and they called for a dictatorship, either the dictatorship of one man or that of 'hands wisely and resolutely revolutionary'."
- The German communist Weitling and the French revolutionary Blanqui borrowed this concept of dictatorship from the Babouvists. Weitling envisaged a personal dictatorship, that of 'a new messiah,' Blanqui, closer to the working-class movement, spoke of a 'Parisian dictatorship' - though Blanqui believed the proletariat was only capable of exercising this dictatorship in the person of one man (him and his homies). 
- Marx and Engels, though likely opposed to Blanquists' concepts, "in 1850 made them the concession of adopting the famous formula" - but they didn't differentiate their non-vanguardist interpretation of the "dictatorship of the proletariat" clearly enough from the Blanquists!

> Later Lenin, claiming to adhere both to Jacobinism and Marxism, would invent the concept of the dictatorship of one party substituting itself for the working class and acting in its name. And his disciples in the Urals, taking his logic to its ultimate conclusion, frankly proclaimed, without being disavowed, that the dictatorship of the proletariat would be a dictatorship over the proletariat.

> ...we must be wary of those who invoke the pretext of “competence” to legitimize the exclusive and abusive use of bourgeois expertise during a revolutionary period.

> During a revolution a man lacking in competency but devoted body and soul to the people’s cause, when he assumes civil or military responsibilities is worth more than a competent individual ready to betray. 

- parvenu: a person who has suddenly risen to a higher social and economic class and has not yet gained social acceptance by others in that class.
- demagogically: in a way that tries to win support by exciting the emotions of ordinary people rather than by having good or morally right ideas.

> Trotsky, incompletely informed, is not totally correct when he asserts that Stalinism “had no prehistory,” that the French Revolution knew nothing that resembled the Soviet bureaucracy, derived from a single revolutionary party and having its roots in the collective ownership of the means of production. I think, on the contrary, that the Hébertist plebeians were, in more ways than one, a foreshadowing of the Russian bureaucrats of the Stalinist era.

- "theoreticians" concentrated essentially on two great problems from the French Revolution: that of permanent revolution and that of the state.
  - first, because the revolution had been only bourgeois, it betrayed popular aspirations, and needed to be continued until the complete liberation of man -- from this, they deduced socialism.
  - some also discovered that within the revolution a new type of people power, oriented from the bottom up, had made its appearance but it had finally been supplanted by a top-down oppressive aparratus. they wondered how to avoid seeing their revolution commandeered in the future -- from this, they deduced anarchism.

> In conclusion, the French Revolution was the source of two great currents of socialist thought which, across the twentieth century, have lasted until today: an authoritarian Jacobin current and a libertarian current. One, of a “bourgeois disposition,” oriented from the top down, is above all concerned with revolutionary effectiveness and claims to be taking account of “necessities.”  The other, of an essentially proletarian spirit, is oriented from the bottom up, and places the safeguarding of freedom to the fore. 

> Bakunin’s anarchist collectivism attempted to reconcile Proudhon and Marx. Within the First International, Marxism sought a middle way between Blanqui and Bakunin. The Commune of 1871 was an empirical synthesis of Jacobinism and federalism. Lenin himself, in State and Revolution, was torn between anarchism and state “communism,” between mass spontaneity and Jacobin iron discipline.
